## Project Overview

As an **Aviation Systems Technician with the Royal Canadian Air Force**, I worked in a component maintenance shop at **19 Wing Comox, British Columbia**, performing second-line mechanical maintenance and testing on aircraft components. Much of my work involved **CP-140 Aurora wheel and brake assemblies**, alongside specialty hose manufacturing, hydraulic testing, fluid contamination analysis, and technical inspections.

This experience combined precision mechanical work with controlled maintenance processes, technical publications, inspection standards, and accountability for the quality of completed work. I progressed from performing authorized maintenance tasks to holding **task-specific Level A maintenance release authorizations** within my approved scope.

## 1. CP-140 Aurora Wheel Overhaul

Wheel maintenance was a complete disassembly, inspection, and rebuild process. Work began with the controlled deflation of the high-pressure tire, normally inflated to approximately **180 PSI**, followed by tire removal using specialized shop equipment. The wheel assembly was separated into its rim halves, cleaned, and prepared for inspection and refurbishment.

Specialized work—including bearing race removal and installation, paint stripping and repainting, and non-destructive testing (NDT)—was performed by other authorized shops or personnel. My component-shop responsibilities included disassembly and reassembly, thorough visual and physical inspections, assessing rim condition against the applicable damage limits, and performing permitted minor surface dressing where authorized.

I also **packed wheel bearings with grease**, examined returned components for surface damage—including scratches on newly installed bearing races—and completed wheel assembly tasks in accordance with the applicable technical instructions. As my qualifications progressed, I obtained Level A authorization within the relevant wheel and bearing maintenance categories and inspected work completed by other technicians.

## 2. Aircraft Brake Assembly Overhaul & Testing

CP-140 brake overhaul involved mechanical disassembly, cleaning, inspection, replacement of specified components, reassembly, and hydraulic functional testing. Inspection was primarily visual, supported by tactile checks and the careful use of picks where required. Assessment included obvious physical damage, surface condition, and signs of heat exposure or metal discoloration. Acceptance and rejection decisions were governed by the applicable technical publications and shop procedures.

Rubber sealing components and springs were routinely replaced during the overhaul process. Before fitting hydraulic O-rings, I prepared them by briefly immersing them in the hydraulic fluid used in their operating environment, typically for approximately **five minutes**.

After reassembly, brake assemblies were filled with hydraulic fluid and connected to test equipment. **Dynamic tests** verified application and release of the braking mechanism, while **static tests** maintained pressure for the specified period to check pressure retention and the assembly's ability to remain applied. Testing and inspections were performed against approved work instructions rather than informal judgment.

## 3. Hydraulic & Pneumatic Hose Manufacturing

I manufactured low- and medium-pressure **hydraulic and pneumatic flexible hose assemblies**, working with rubber and wire-braided hose construction and Aeroquip fittings. The process required selecting the appropriate components, preparing and assembling the hose ends, applying specified sealing materials where required, and conducting proof or pressure tests in accordance with the relevant instructions.

Depending on the assembly, testing pressures reached approximately **3,000 PSI**. Completed hoses received identification and service tags documenting their manufacture and test status. The work required careful control of assembly details, connection integrity, pressure-test results, and traceability.

## 4. Hydraulic Fluid Contamination Analysis

I collected hydraulic fluid samples from **aircraft and hydraulic test units** and tested them using a **Parker icount LaserCM20 portable particle counter**. Testing was conducted under semi-clean-room conditions to reduce the possibility of introducing contamination during sample handling.

My responsibilities included operating the equipment, interpreting the particle-count results against the applicable fluid cleanliness criteria, and documenting the findings. If an initial sample failed its requirements, a fresh sample was collected and retested. A repeated failure required escalation and the affected aircraft was **quarantined pending further investigation** under the applicable maintenance process.

I also participated in **blind equipment correlation testing**. The shop received samples from a national testing centre without being told the expected particle counts. We tested the samples, submitted the measured results, and received a pass/fail determination from the centre. This was an external check of the reliability of our measurements, rather than a claim that I performed instrument calibration.

## 5. Technical Inspection & Maintenance Certification

My maintenance qualifications included **Performance of Maintenance (POM)** and, later, **Level A maintenance release authorization** for defined aircraft/component maintenance categories. These authorizations were **specific to the applicable tasks, equipment, and aircraft context**—not blanket authority to release an entire aircraft to flight.

Within my approved scope, I inspected critical maintenance junctures, verified completed work against technical instructions and standard operating procedures, and certified maintenance tasks performed by other technicians. The transition from hands-on task performance to inspection and certification increased my responsibility for maintenance quality, traceability, and technical compliance.

## 6. Technical Initiative & Process Improvement

**Tailored maintenance training aid (2015).** While mentoring a newly assigned technician, I developed a supplementary checklist tailored to the individual's needs and based on established maintenance procedures. The technician adopted the reference, became more independent, and reduced repeated errors and requests for assistance. My contemporaneous records estimate that the checklist reduced the **supervision time required for that trainee by approximately 75%**. It was a targeted training improvement, not a replacement for approved technical instructions.

**Repurposed brake assembly for training (2014).** Working with a colleague, I requested and obtained approval from the applicable lifecycle material management authority to convert a condemned CP-140 brake assembly into a training aid. This provided a practical opportunity to support component familiarization without using a serviceable asset.

**Maintenance tooling documentation (2014).** I drafted a special instruction describing the use of a maintenance safety-retention tool and contributed practical information to a proposed technical-publication amendment.

**Time-sensitive brake assembly work (2014).** Working with a colleague, I completed multiple brake assemblies while a required, difficult-to-source sealant remained within its permitted shelf life, helping sustain component maintenance output while respecting material controls.

## Technical Outcomes

This role strengthened my practical experience in **mechanical component overhaul, hydraulic and pneumatic systems, dimensional and condition-based inspection, pressure testing, contamination control, technical documentation, and maintenance quality assurance**.

A particularly important progression was moving from hands-on maintenance to **task-specific Level A inspection and certification**, while also supporting junior technicians through training and practical job aids. These are skills I continue to apply in mechanical design, equipment troubleshooting, technical evaluation, and engineering technology work.
