# Inventory Operations Architecture & Systems Design

**Microsoft Access · Storage-location architecture · Operational process improvement · Change management**

*Turning an informal, person-dependent inventory arrangement into a more searchable and accountable way of finding, receiving, and using materials.*

![Illustrative design linking searchable inventory records with physical bin storage.]({{ '/assets/images/inventory-operations-systems-design/inventory-system-overview.svg' | relative_url }})

*Conceptual illustration. Item descriptions, quantities, and location codes shown are fictional—not a screenshot of company data.*

## The challenge

As an industrial engineering and maintenance operation expanded, spare parts and materials were spread across storage rooms, cabinets, shelves, and staging areas. Finding a part could depend on asking one of the few employees who remembered where it had been put. Even when stock was physically available, it was not always easy to verify its existence or locate it quickly.

This created two avoidable costs: **employee time spent searching**, including time that engineering and shop staff could have used for other work, and **repeat purchases of material already in inventory** when available stock was overlooked.

## My contribution

I helped establish a practical inventory-control system that connected a **Microsoft Access inventory database** with labelled physical storage locations and clearer employee procedures. My contributions included inventory organization and tracking, the use of searchable records and location-specific listings, developing and explaining the storage-location logic, and delivering training materials to support adoption.

The challenge was not simply entering part data; it was making the electronic system understandable and usable by people handling materials every day.

## System design: from item search to physical location

### A searchable inventory register

Microsoft Access provided searchable item listings, descriptions, on-hand quantities, and locations. Location-specific queries allowed staff to inspect stock within a particular area, while partial-text searches helped identify a part even if someone did not know the exact identifier.

### A structured storage-location framework

Instead of relying on descriptions such as "somewhere in the cabinet," the system used progressively more specific location information to identify an area and the item's position within it. This approach was supported by visible labels on shelving, cabinets, and bins and by physical reorganization where necessary.

### Receiving, purchasing, and consumption processes

To remain useful, a database must reflect what actually moves through the shop. Employee guidance connected the register to purchasing checks, goods receipt, staging, and recording inventory use. In practical terms, the expectations included checking existing stock before ordering, inspecting and documenting deliveries, keeping receiving paperwork with materials, assigning locations, and recording what was taken from inventory.

## Change management: turning procedures into habits

**Employee adoption was an essential part of making the new inventory system work.** A searchable database alone would not prevent lost paperwork, unrecorded withdrawals, or items being set down in an unexpected location. The process needed to be understood and followed consistently.

I prepared an employee presentation explaining the reasons for the change, the benefits of the new organization, how to search the inventory listings, and how to interpret location information. A memorable part of the training was a series of staged skits: **"The Good," "The Bad," and "The Ugly."**

The skits contrasted the correct process with exaggerated examples of mistakes and shortcuts, including scenarios informed by problems that had genuinely happened in the workplace. By using humour rather than reading procedures word-for-word, the presentation reinforced the desired behaviours and gave employees a practical reason to follow the new system.

This work combined **technical implementation, communication, and change management**: explaining why old habits were costly, demonstrating better ways of working, and encouraging a shared standard across roles.

## Operational results: time savings and cost avoidance

The new approach made inventory more accessible and helped employees avoid unnecessary searching and duplicate ordering. By making both existing stock and its location easier to verify, it reduced reliance on informal knowledge and helped engineers, technicians, and procurement staff make better-informed decisions before purchasing.

**The practical benefits were labour-time savings, avoided unnecessary material purchases, improved material traceability, and clearer accountability.** These advantages translated into genuine cost avoidance for the operation. Exact dollar savings, labour hours, and percentage improvements were not formally measured, so they are not presented as quantified financial results.

## Employee training video — planned feature

A short, 1980s/1990s action-movie-style trailer has been developed privately from the staged **Good / Bad / Ugly** receiving skits. The trailer contrasts improper handling with the correct process, using humour to show how employee training supported the change-management effort.

## What the project demonstrates

This was a practical systems-design project spanning **database access, physical inventory architecture, procedures, operational efficiency, and human behaviour**. Its value came from connecting those elements so that employees could find items, understand the process, and keep the records useful over time.
