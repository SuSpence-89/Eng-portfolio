## Project Overview

During my service with the Royal Canadian Air Force at the Aerospace Engineering Test Establishment (AETE) in Cold Lake, I developed specialized experience operating industrial laser-marking equipment.

The work involved an **RMI Laser marking system** housed within a large enclosed workstation. The laser is believed to have been an **RMI UF-50 fibre laser marker**, although the exact model remains to be confirmed.

As my experience with the equipment developed, I became a **go-to resource for laser marking and engraving**, particularly for projects that required experimentation, custom artwork, or adjustments beyond previously established settings.

My involvement extended beyond operating the equipment.

I regularly experimented with marking parameters, evaluated the results on different materials, and developed reusable programs for successful applications.

The projects ranged from practical equipment identification to decorative engraving, commemorative plaques, and experimental marking on curved metallic surfaces.

## 1. Equipment & Laser-Marking Systems

The laser-marking work was performed using industrial equipment manufactured by **RMI Laser**.

### Equipment Configuration

- **Manufacturer:** RMI Laser
- **Laser system:** Industrial fibre laser marker
- **Probable model:** RMI UF-50 (pending confirmation)
- **Workstation:** RMI Large/Extra Large enclosed laser workstation
- **Programming:** RMI laser-marking software using SW7 project files
- **Applications:** Equipment identification, material marking, graphics, and personalized engraving

The enclosed workstation provided a controlled environment for positioning workpieces and carrying out laser-marking operations.

Projects required attention to workpiece positioning, marking dimensions, surface characteristics, and the relationship between the material surface and the laser's focal position.

Different workpieces presented different challenges, particularly when moving between flat metallic surfaces, coated materials, plastics, and curved objects.

## 2. Laser-Marking Parameter Development

A significant part of my experience involved **independently developing and refining laser-marking parameters**.

Rather than relying exclusively on established settings, I was willing to experiment when a new material or application produced an unsatisfactory result.

My testing involved adjusting several machine parameters.

| Parameter | Development Consideration |
|---|---|
| Laser power | Adjusting the energy applied during marking |
| Marking speed | Evaluating the relationship between travel speed and the resulting mark |
| Pulse frequency | Experimenting with pulse delivery and surface response |
| Number of pulses | Adjusting programmed pulse settings for the application |
| Number of passes | Evaluating whether additional passes improved the desired result |
| Focal distance | Investigating changes in marking behaviour associated with the laser's position relative to the workpiece |

I evaluated the resulting marks based on their appearance, contrast, readability, and suitability for the intended application.

When the results were unsatisfactory, I adjusted the relevant parameters and repeated the process.

This iterative approach helped me develop a practical understanding of how different materials responded to the laser.

### Preserving Successful Programs

When I achieved a successful marking result, I saved the corresponding program and settings for future reference.

This was particularly useful for applications involving specific materials or surface finishes.

Saving successful programs reduced the need to repeat the entire experimental process when similar work was requested again.

Over time, this created a collection of material-specific and application-specific marking files.

My surviving **SW7 project files** document examples of this work, including marking parameters, text objects, graphics, and material-specific programs.

These files provide supporting technical records of the programming and experimentation undertaken.

## 3. Material-Specific Laser Marking

One of the most important lessons from my experience was that successful laser marking depended on more than simply identifying the base material.

Surface characteristics, coatings, colour, and geometry could significantly affect the appearance of the finished mark.

### Plastic Marking & Colour Differences

While experimenting with plastic components, I discovered that **different colours of the same general material could respond differently to the laser**.

For example, I found that red plastics were often more difficult to mark successfully than green or blue plastics.

A program that produced a satisfactory mark on one colour could require further adjustments when applied to another.

This reinforced the importance of testing the actual workpiece material and finish rather than assuming that one setting would be suitable for every variation.

My saved programs include examples associated with plastic ear defenders and other plastic equipment components.

### Metallic Surfaces

I also worked with metallic items and identification components.

These projects required consideration of the desired contrast, marking appearance, surface finish, and workpiece geometry.

Applications included engraved identification tags, decorative metallic objects, and personalized markings.

### Coated & Painted Materials

Coated surfaces provided another opportunity to experiment with different marking conditions.

The interaction between the marking process and the surface coating influenced the resulting appearance.

My saved project files include programs developed for painted or coated metallic components.

### Other Material Trials

I also experimented with programs associated with materials such as wood, rubber, and stone.

These material trials formed part of my broader effort to understand the capabilities and limitations of the equipment.

Not every experimental program represented a finalized production process; testing and learning were important parts of the work.

## 4. Featured Case Study — Multicolour Laser Marking on Curved Brass

One of my more interesting experiments involved creating a detailed **knight, sword, and shield emblem on a polished brass casing**.

The project presented two particular challenges:

1. The marking surface was curved rather than flat.
2. I wanted to produce a visually distinctive result with multiple colours rather than a conventional single-colour engraving.

### Curved-Surface Challenge

Unlike a flat workpiece, a cylindrical surface does not maintain a constant distance from the laser across the entire marking area.

As the marking position moves across the curvature, the relationship between the laser's focal position and the material surface changes.

I experimented with this characteristic rather than treating it exclusively as a limitation.

### Experimental Approach

I adjusted the marking conditions and experimented with the **distance between the laser and the curved brass surface**.

By exploring how these changes affected the appearance of the mark, I was able to produce different colours within the engraved artwork.

The finished emblem displayed combinations of:

- Blue
- Purple
- Red
- Orange

The colour variations were produced during the laser-marking process rather than through the application of coloured ink or paint.

### Result

The finished piece featured a recognizable knight-and-shield emblem with multiple colours visible against the polished brass background.

The experiment demonstrated that changes in marking conditions and workpiece geometry could be used creatively to influence the final appearance.

This project was particularly valuable because it combined **process experimentation, curved-surface marking, artwork reproduction, and visual quality assessment**.

It also reinforced the importance of testing and observing the actual material response rather than relying solely on predetermined machine settings.

## 5. Raster Images & Custom Artwork

In addition to lettering and identification markings, I experimented with **raster-image laser engraving**.

These projects involved working with image-based artwork and preparing marking programs capable of reproducing graphical details on physical surfaces.

Image engraving introduced different considerations from conventional text marking.

These included:

- Artwork size and placement.
- Image detail and visual contrast.
- Reproduction of fine features.
- Material response to the programmed marking conditions.
- Overall appearance of the finished graphic.

The work required experimentation to determine whether the selected settings could reproduce the desired visual result.

My saved SW7 files also include projects containing combinations of image and text objects.

This experience expanded my use of the equipment beyond basic equipment identification into custom decorative and graphical applications.

## 9. Troubleshooting & Process Improvement

The most valuable part of this experience was developing the ability to approach unfamiliar marking problems systematically.

When an initial result was unsuccessful, I investigated possible contributing factors rather than assuming the material could not be marked.

These factors included:

**Material response:** Determining whether the material or coating required a different marking approach.

**Colour variation:** Recognizing that differently coloured plastics could behave differently even when their general material type was similar.

**Marking parameters:** Adjusting speed, power, frequency, pulses, and passes to improve the result.

**Surface geometry:** Accounting for changes in distance and focal relationship when marking curved components.

**Artwork characteristics:** Considering whether the desired image detail, scale, or layout was suitable for the application.

**Repeatability:** Saving successful configurations so that future projects could benefit from previous experimentation.

As my familiarity with the equipment grew, I became a resource for others when laser-marking projects required specialized attention or additional experimentation.

This experience reinforced the importance of **controlled testing, observation, technical judgment, and documentation** when developing practical manufacturing processes.

## Project Outcome

My experience with the RMI laser-marking system developed from equipment operation into a broader capability involving **process experimentation, material-specific marking, custom fabrication, and reusable program development**.

I became a go-to resource for laser-marking tasks and was particularly interested in exploring applications that required adjustments beyond established settings.

The work demonstrated that successful laser marking depends on understanding the relationship between **equipment parameters, material properties, surface geometry, and the desired result**.

The multicolour engraving on curved brass is a particularly strong example of this approach: experimentation with machine settings and focal distance produced a distinctive finished marking that would not have been achieved simply by applying a standard preset.

This experience strengthened my ability to troubleshoot manufacturing processes, evaluate material behaviour, develop practical solutions, and document successful methods for future use.
