Engineering projects depend on much more than sound technical designs. Specialized components, prototype equipment, and workshop materials must also reach the right people—sometimes across provincial borders, oceans, multiple carriers, and international customs systems.

In support of rail-equipment engineering and workshop operations, I coordinated inbound and outbound technical shipments throughout Canada and internationally. The role connected suppliers, engineers, freight forwarders, carriers, customs brokers, warehouse and receiving personnel, and other stakeholders. My responsibility was to keep information moving between those parties and work through the practical complications that arise when equipment crosses borders and changes hands.

## Scope of responsibility

My work included coordinating freight quotations and shipment arrangements; communicating dimensions, weights, technical descriptions, pickup and delivery requirements; collecting and routing supporting commercial and customs documentation; following up with carriers and brokers; and keeping shipping and receiving stakeholders informed as a shipment progressed.

A Canadian freight move and a shipment crossing the U.S.–Mexico border require very different approaches. Shipment-specific considerations could involve carrier availability, time zones, equipment suitability, loading and unloading arrangements, customs-release status, origin and tariff documentation, and the requirements of the parties responsible for import and delivery. I also had to account for the **2020 Incoterms®** framework, which sets out the agreed division of delivery, transport-cost, and risk responsibilities between buyer and seller. The specific term and named delivery point matter: coordinating freight does not necessarily mean that the same party is responsible for every leg, charge, or customs formality. I learned to treat **transportation, documentation, and receiving readiness as connected parts of one operation**, rather than separate administrative tasks.

## Project example 1 — China to British Columbia: ocean freight and inland delivery

One particularly involved assignment was coordinating an overseas shipment originating in **China**, arriving by **ocean freight in the Delta, British Columbia, port area**, and then moving onward to **Prince George**.

This was not a single-carrier, door-to-door parcel delivery. The operation involved an international ocean movement, arrival and receiving arrangements at the Canadian port/dock stage, and a separate inland freight handoff to reach the final destination. I coordinated between the relevant parties to keep the overseas and Canadian portions of the shipment aligned.

This assignment illustrates **intermodal transportation**: moving freight through connected transport stages, with careful coordination when cargo or a container transfers from the ocean-freight network to inland carriers. One potential part of the port-side handoff is **drayage**, the typically short-distance truck movement between a marine terminal and another transport or handling point, such as a warehouse or intermodal facility. The shipment required coordination of the ocean arrival and subsequent inland freight; the available records do not yet establish the precise drayage arrangement or the modes used on every inland leg.

The shipment highlights the importance of understanding how an ocean delivery is handed over for domestic transport. The supporting ocean-freight workflow reference sets out the information that commonly must be aligned at these interfaces: shipping terms, commercial invoice and packing list, bill of lading, vessel and arrival notices, cargo-control references, broker documentation, freight-release status, container or cargo availability, pickup arrangements, and receiving records.

**What this demonstrates:** intermodal freight coordination, supplier and forwarder communication, port-to-inland handoffs, drayage planning awareness, and the documentation dependencies that can affect release and delivery.

## Project example 3 — Canada, Laredo, and Mexico: cross-border problem-solving

A documented U.S.–Mexico freight assignment involved coordinating a heavy, high-cube container movement from Alberta toward a freight and customs interface in **Laredo, Texas**. My correspondence includes a request for transportation pricing and the necessary cargo information, alongside an extended group discussion involving carrier, forwarding, and receiving parties.

The complexity became apparent as the shipment progressed. Participants identified an **import restriction affecting a helical gearmotor assembly** entering Mexico and discussed the time required for NOM-related documentation. In parallel, the logistics team raised concerns about the timing and documentation associated with a bonded movement. The thread also shows the need to confirm bonded-warehouse information and exchange border-crossing and customs evidence between the appropriate parties.

My contribution was part of the **coordination and communication effort**, including freight-quotation activity and directing questions to parties positioned to provide the necessary border or import information. Regulatory decisions and customs entry actions remained with the appropriate parties; the record does not establish that I personally obtained a regulatory approval or completed the ultimate delivery into Mexico.

**What this demonstrates:** escalation of cross-border obstacles, awareness of customs and bond-related timing risk, careful communication of technical cargo information, and coordination among multiple organizations under schedule pressure.

## Canada-wide technical freight and regional requirements

Alongside these international assignments, I coordinated shipments to and from the railshop and facilities in different parts of Canada. These moves required working across provincial boundaries and time zones while aligning the needs of shop personnel, suppliers, dispatchers, and receiving locations.

Because the cargo supported technical work, successful coordination meant more than selecting a delivery date. ## Freight terms, delivery responsibilities, and transport modes

A central part of coordinating international freight was understanding where one organization’s responsibility ended and another’s began. **Incoterms® 2020** provide internationally recognized delivery terms—such as EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), FOB (Free On Board), DAP (Delivered at Place), and DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)—that clarify the allocation of certain costs, transport arrangements, customs formalities, and risk of loss. The selected term must be considered together with its **named place or port**; it does not by itself establish legal ownership of the goods. The project documents demonstrate familiarity with these terms, although they do not establish which specific Incoterm governed each example shipment.

I also drew on freight terminology to communicate effectively with carriers and receiving teams. **Intermodal transportation** describes freight moving across connected modes, commonly in a container that can be transferred between ship, rail, and truck without unpacking its contents. **Drayage** is the shorter trucking connection typically used around ports, terminals, and intermodal yards. These concepts are especially relevant to the China–British Columbia–Prince George shipment and help explain why booking the ocean crossing alone was only part of the coordination effort.

## The documentation side of international shipping

An important part of the work was recognizing when the shipping documentation required additional attention, then gathering accurate information and communicating with the responsible freight, brokerage, and commercial parties.

The supporting materials I retained include **commercial and pro forma invoice formats**, **USMCA/CUSMA certificates of origin and low-value statements**, **customs-broker authorization and continuous-bond documents**, **CBP entry-summary references**, **EPA/FDA disclaimer and TSCA certification templates**, and a **draft request for a U.S. binding tariff-classification ruling** involving specialized railway lifting-equipment components.

These materials show the range of customs and trade-compliance interfaces relevant to industrial equipment shipments. Not every document applied to every movement; some are templates or historical company reference records, and the classification-ruling request is a draft rather than an issued ruling. My role was to support the flow of accurate shipment and technical information, **not to act as a licensed customs broker or independently certify regulatory compliance**.

## A practical shipment-coordination workflow

A related international-shipping work-instruction reference organizes the work into ocean and air freight, with separate import and export processes. It provides a useful framework for explaining the coordination involved in a technical shipment:

1. **Define the cargo and responsibilities:** Gather technical descriptions, quantities, weights, dimensions, handling needs, ready dates, and the applicable **Incoterms® 2020 rule and named delivery point**, where agreed.
2. **Arrange the transportation:** Coordinate with the supplier, freight forwarder, ocean carrier, airline, or ground carrier; establish the planned **intermodal** route, terminal handoffs, and any required **drayage** arrangements.
3. **Align the documentation:** Confirm relevant invoices, packing lists, bills of lading or air waybills, origin information, and shipment-specific supporting records.
4. **Coordinate arrival and release:** Track the shipment and relay required information to the customs broker, terminal, receiving party, and downstream carrier.
5. **Complete the delivery handoff:** Confirm cargo availability and receiving arrangements, and communicate discrepancies or missing information for resolution.
6. **Close the loop:** Reconcile shipment details, communicate delivery status, and preserve appropriate records for follow-up.

This is a **process reference**, not a claim that every listed step was performed on every shipment or that the work instruction was formally adopted as company policy.

## Why engineering knowledge mattered

Industrial rail equipment is not always easy to describe, package, lift, or transport. Understanding the item’s function and technical context helped me communicate more effectively with carriers and brokers about cargo descriptions, equipment limitations, handling considerations, and questions about how specialized parts fit into a larger machine or system.

The role required switching between two perspectives: the engineering team’s need for the right component or prototype, and the logistics team’s need for accurate, actionable shipping information. Bridging those perspectives reduced ambiguity and helped participants recognize issues before the next handoff.

## Value and professional development

This work supported engineering and workshop operations by connecting the technical, transportation, customs, and receiving sides of the supply chain. It required follow-through, detailed record awareness, clear communication, and the judgment to escalate problems to the organizations responsible for resolving them.

The experience strengthened my capability in **technical logistics coordination, international stakeholder communication, cross-border documentation, intermodal transportation and drayage interfaces, Incoterms® 2020 awareness, and practical operational problem-solving**. Because I do not have a complete validated performance dataset, I have not attributed numerical savings, shipment counts, delivery success rates, or regulatory outcomes to this project.
